## People and historical figures

Separate five questions that are often collapsed into "what did they look
like?":

1. **Identity**: whether the remains, portrait, description, or object actually
   belongs to the named person.
2. **Facial structure**: what photographs, life portraits, masks, scans, or
   skeletal anatomy can constrain.
3. **Pigmentation and hair**: what photographs, preserved tissue, contemporary
   descriptions, or appropriately interpreted genetic evidence can constrain.
4. **Dress and grooming**: what date, place, gender role, status, occupation,
   activity, season, portrait convention, and material evidence support.
5. **Characterization**: expression, gesture, personality, cleanliness, scars,
   tattoos, and private details that may remain illustrative.

Use authority in this order:

- securely identified photographs, film, life portraits, casts, or death
  masks, while accounting for retouching and portrait purpose;
- securely associated remains analyzed by appropriate specialists;
- contemporary written descriptions reconciled for bias and genre;
- securely dated clothing, jewellery, tools, and iconography from the same
  context;
- regional biological, textile, and material evidence;
- broad analogy, kept non-focal and labelled.

Call a skull-based result a `facial approximation`, not a recovered exact face.
Skull form can constrain some proportions; soft-tissue thickness, ears, lips,
hair, pigmentation, fat distribution, expression, and age-related appearance
remain variably constrained. DNA-derived appearance predictions must retain
their reported uncertainty and do not establish personality, social identity,
or a complete likeness.

Do not infer appearance from modern nationality, a broad archaeological
culture, a language label, or a generic image-search average. Avoid one
"representative" face for an entire population.

For an unnamed ordinary person, define a plausible role and context without
inventing a personal biography. Say `a plausible dock worker in...`, not `this
is how the dock worker looked`.

## Objects and clothing

Lock:

- measured dimensions and proportions;
- material, manufacturing technique, joins, fasteners, and surface treatment;
- preserved colour, residue, wear, breakage, repair, and use traces;
- which portions survive and which are restored by symmetry, typology, text,
  iconography, or analogy;
- how the object is held, worn, installed, transported, or used.

Do not make incomplete objects look factory-new without disclosing restored
sections. If colour or missing geometry changes the object's identity, create
matched variants.

For clothing, distinguish:

- surviving textile or garment;
- visual sources and their artistic conventions;
- written terms whose exact cut or fibre may be debated;
- regional textile technology;
- status or ceremonial dress versus ordinary workwear;
- modern costume reproductions used only as analogies.

Check that layers, closures, drape, tool access, movement, weather, and labour
are physically plausible.

## Buildings and interiors

Freeze the phase before reconstructing. Lock:

- surveyed or excavated footprint and wall sequence;
- topography, orientation, access, circulation, floor levels, and drainage;
- structural spans, wall thicknesses, roof loads, openings, and construction
  sequence;
- attested materials, finishes, colour, fixtures, fuel, light, water, storage,
  refuse, repair, and reuse;
- relationships to adjacent structures and landscape.

Treat elevation, roof form, openings, upper storeys, interior partitions,
decoration, and furnishing as separate decisions; a firm footprint does not
prove them.

Use architecture and art-history comparanda only after recording the
comparison's distance in date, place, function, status, and building tradition.
Reject aesthetically tidy geometry that conflicts with surveyed irregularity.

## Settlements and cities

Choose the image's purpose and scale:

- regional setting;
- aerial urban morphology;
- neighbourhood or street sequence;
- building group;
- street-level lived scene;
- infrastructure or production cutaway.

At city scale, establish:

- shoreline, rivers, relief, vegetation, and period climate;
- wall, road, plot, canal, harbour, market, institutional, production,
  funerary, and waste systems;
- successive phases, destruction, contraction, rebuilding, and reuse;
- settlement density and where evidence is absent;
- building-height ranges and roofscape;
- movement of people, animals, goods, water, fuel, and waste;
- ordinary margins as well as monumental centres.

A documented street plan does not prove simultaneous occupation or matching
elevations. Show uncertain districts at a coarser level or provide variants.
Do not populate every open space or make the city either implausibly empty or
cinematically overcrowded.

## Lived scenes and landscapes

Reconstruct the physical process, not a costume tableau. Ask:

- What task is occurring, in what sequence, with which skill and labour?
- Which tools, materials, by-products, hazards, and waste does it require?
- What season, weather, daylight, food, fuel, water, flora, and fauna fit?
- Who is present, absent, permitted, excluded, or represented only by an elite
  source?
- Which sounds, smells, crowding, repairs, disease, disability, or age ranges
  would affect the visible scene?

Use environmental archaeology, zooarchaeology, archaeobotany, human
osteology, residue analysis, experimental archaeology, and contemporary
records where relevant. Do not use environmental context as decorative
background.

When evidence is thin, select ordinary low-salience details and keep invented
narrative moments out of the focal point. A plausible scene should be lively,
but its drama must not create a false historical claim.
